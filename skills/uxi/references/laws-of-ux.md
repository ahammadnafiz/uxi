# Laws of UX, all thirty, with judgment attached

Read this when you need the psychology behind a design decision, or a name for the effect you are seeing. Every entry has the claim, where it comes from, what to do with it, and where people misuse it. The misuse column matters as much as the law, because most of these get quoted as trump cards in arguments they do not settle.

The collection and naming follow Jon Yablonski's Laws of UX (lawsofux.com, CC BY-NC-ND). The principles themselves are older science, cited per entry. Everything here is restated in this skill's own words.

## Perception and attention

**Law of proximity.** Things placed near each other read as related. Gestalt psychology, from Wertheimer's work starting around 1910. Your primary grouping tool: adjust spacing before you add a border, a card, or a label. Most screens that feel cluttered have a spacing problem, not a content problem. Misuse: cramming unrelated controls together because space is tight, which tells the eye a lie about their relationship.

**Law of common region.** A shared visible boundary groups items even more strongly than nearness does. Also Gestalt. This is why cards work, and why a background tint can group a form section without a single line of copy. Misuse: boxes inside boxes inside boxes. Each container spends ink; when everything is in a region, regions stop meaning anything.

**Law of similarity.** Elements that share color, shape, size, or motion read as one class with one behavior, even when scattered. Gestalt. Use it to make every clickable thing look clickable and every non-clickable thing look inert. The direct consequence: text styled like a link that is not a link is a bug, and a real link styled like body text is a hidden feature. Misuse: reusing the accent color for decoration, which quietly teaches people that the accent means nothing.

**Law of uniform connectedness.** A drawn connection (a line, an arrow, a shared frame) binds elements more strongly than proximity or similarity. Gestalt. Use it for steps in a flow, for linking a control to the thing it controls, and for showing which label owns which value in a dense chart. Misuse: connector soup in diagrams, where every line weakens every other line.

**Law of Prägnanz.** People resolve an ambiguous or complex image into the simplest shape it could be, because the simple reading costs the least. Wertheimer again. Simple figures are processed and remembered better than ornate ones, and ambiguity never survives, it just gets resolved without you. If a layout could be read two ways, some of your users are reading it the wrong way right now. Misuse: treating this as a mandate for minimalism. It is about ambiguity, not about how many things are on screen.

**Selective attention.** People filter hard to whatever serves the current goal and genuinely do not perceive the rest. Attention research from Cherry's cocktail party work (1953) and Broadbent's filter model (1958) onward. Banner blindness is this: anything shaped like an ad, placed where ads live, is invisible, including your own content. Change blindness is this too: an update the interface makes quietly, while attention is elsewhere, did not happen as far as the person is concerned. If something must be noticed, put it in the goal path and give it a strong cue, and do not change two things at once. Misuse: concluding people are careless. They are filtering, which is the whole reason they can use your product at all.

**Von Restorff effect.** In a field of similar items, the one that differs is the one remembered. Hedwig von Restorff, 1933. This is the mechanics behind one primary action per state: a single distinct button is memorable and findable, two distinct buttons are just noise. Emphasis is a budget, spend it in one place. Two cautions that come with it: never make the distinction color-only, and be careful using motion as the distinguisher for people with motion sensitivity.

## Memory and cognition

**Miller's law.** Popularly, seven plus or minus two items in immediate memory, from George Miller's 1956 paper. The part everyone forgets is that Miller's real point was chunking, and the number was closer to a rhetorical device than a design limit. Later work (Cowan and others) puts practical working memory nearer four chunks. Use it to justify grouping, never to justify "menus must have seven items," which is the single most common misquote in design reviews.

**Chunking.** Breaking an information set into meaningful groups makes it scannable, processable, and memorable. Same Miller lineage. Phone numbers, card numbers grouped in fours, settings pages with sections, and stat blocks are all chunking. The test of a good chunk is that it has a name: if you cannot label a group, it is probably not a real group.

**Working memory.** The system that holds and manipulates task information is small (about four chunks, three to five in practice) and fast to fade (twenty to thirty seconds per chunk without rehearsal). Term from Miller, Galanter, and Pribram in the 1960s; Atkinson and Shiffrin's short-term store; modern treatment by Baddeley and Cowan. This is the science behind the carried-state score in the main skill: every ID to copy, filter to reapply, or value to remember across a navigation is a chunk spent from a four-chunk budget. Put the burden on the system. Mark what has been visited, carry values forward, and make comparisons side by side instead of from memory.

**Cognitive load.** The total mental resource a task demands. Sweller's cognitive load theory, late 1980s. The useful split: intrinsic load is the difficulty of the task itself, and extraneous load is everything the interface adds on top: decoding an unfamiliar layout, ignoring decoration, translating jargon. You often cannot reduce intrinsic load, and you can almost always reduce extraneous load. That is where design effort pays.

**Cognitive bias.** Judgment runs on shortcuts, and the shortcuts have systematic failure modes. Tversky and Kahneman, from 1972 onward. So design for how people actually decide, not for an idealized rational user. And audit your own process, because confirmation bias reads every test session as validating the design you already made. Knowing about a bias does not remove it, but it lets you build the check: write down what would change your mind before you look at the data.

**Mental model.** People carry a compressed model of how a system works, built from every similar system they have used, and they act on the model rather than on your implementation. Kenneth Craik, 1943. When the design matches the model, everything feels obvious; when it fights the model, every step needs explaining. Your model of the system is nothing like your users' model, and the gap between the two is what research methods (interviews, card sorts, journey maps) exist to measure. Misuse: assuming the team's model is the users' model because everyone in the room agrees.

**Zeigarnik effect.** Interrupted and incomplete tasks are remembered better than finished ones. Bluma Zeigarnik, 1920s. The honest uses: resumable flows, saved drafts, visible signifiers that there is more content past the fold, and progress states that pull people back to finish. The dishonest use is manufactured incompleteness as an engagement hook, and it belongs in the ethics file, not the design.

**Serial position effect.** First and last items in a sequence are recalled best (primacy and recency), the middle worst. Ebbinghaus. Put the actions that matter at the ends of a navigation and let the middle carry the less critical items. Pairs with the peak-end rule: position shapes memory, so position is a design material.

## Decision and motivation

**Hick's law.** Decision time grows with the number and complexity of choices. Hick and Hyman, 1952. Cut options where response time matters, split complex decisions into steps, and mark a recommended option so people can defer the full comparison. The two cautions the law itself carries: it describes equally weighted unfamiliar choices, so a long alphabetical list of known items does not behave the same, and simplifying past the point of clarity (mystery-meat icons, buried settings) trades decision time for search time, which is worse.

**Choice overload.** Past a point, more options slow the decision, then degrade it and sour the whole experience, sometimes ending in no choice at all. Term from Toffler's Future Shock (1970); the jam-study literature made it famous, with the honest caveat that replications show the effect depends on context. Practical moves: curate before you display, feature a default, give filters and search up front, and when a comparison is genuinely required (pricing tiers), lay it out side by side so nobody holds options in memory.

**Goal-gradient effect.** Effort accelerates as the goal gets close. Clark Hull, 1932, rats first, loyalty cards later. Honest uses: show real progress, give a running start where one truthfully exists (two of eight steps done because account data already covers them), and keep the last step the lightest. The endowed-progress version, where the head start is invented, sits on the ethics line: it measurably increases completion, and it is a small lie. Decide deliberately, do not drift into it.

**Peak-end rule.** People remember an experience by its most intense moment and its ending, not by the average. Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber, and Redelmeier, 1993. Budget craft accordingly: the confirmation, the first success, and the worst error deserve more design than the middle of the flow. Remember the asymmetry, negative peaks are recalled more vividly than positive ones, so the highest-return work is usually fixing the worst moment, not gilding the best one.

**Flow.** Full absorption in a task happens when challenge and skill are balanced, feedback is immediate, and nothing yanks attention away. Csíkszentmihályi, 1975. Design for it by keeping response times inside the Doherty window, giving continuous quiet feedback, and ruthlessly removing interruptions from the middle of work. Too hard produces frustration, too easy produces boredom, and one modal in the wrong place produces neither, just an exit.

**Paradox of the active user.** People start using software immediately and do not read the manual, even when reading first would save them time overall. Rosson and Carroll, 1987. This is a finding about motivation, not intelligence: people are here to do their task, not to study your product. Design for it rather than against it: help in context at the moment of need, empty states that teach, defaults that make the unread path safe. A tutorial nobody reads is not documentation, it is a speed bump.

**Parkinson's law.** Work expands to fill the time available for it. C. Northcote Parkinson, 1955, as satire that turned out to generalize. In interfaces: a task tends to consume the time people expect it to take, so beating the expected duration is where delight lives. Autofill, sensible defaults, and remembered inputs collapse expected-minutes into actual-seconds, which people notice and remember.

## Interaction mechanics

**Fitts's law.** Time to acquire a target is a function of distance to it and its size; small far targets are slow and error-prone. Paul Fitts, 1954, with a real equation behind it. Consequences: frequent and important targets get bigger and closer to the point of attention, destructive targets get distance from frequent ones, and on desktop the screen edges and corners are effectively infinite targets because the cursor stops there. This is also the physics under every platform's minimum target size table.

**Doherty threshold.** Keep the loop between human action and system response under about 400 ms and people stay engaged; make them wait and attention unravels. Doherty and Thadani, IBM, 1982, replacing the old two-second standard. Where you cannot hit the number, manage the perception: optimistic UI, skeletons matching final geometry, and progress bars, which make waits tolerable almost regardless of their accuracy. One counterintuitive corollary from the same literature: a result that returns too instantly for a task people believe is hard can read as untrustworthy, and a brief deliberate delay can increase perceived value. Use that one sparingly and honestly.

**Postel's law.** Be liberal in what you accept, conservative in what you send. Jon Postel, from the TCP robustness principle. As interface guidance: accept every reasonable input format (spaces in card numbers, any date order you can disambiguate, pasted text with junk whitespace), normalize silently, and be strict and consistent in what you display back. Every format error you show a user for input you could have parsed is a self-inflicted wound.

**Jakob's law.** People spend most of their time in other products, so they expect yours to work like the ones they already know. Named for Jakob Nielsen. The strongest argument for convention that exists, and the reason "innovative navigation" usually means "navigation nobody can find." The overlooked second half: when you do change an established design, let people cross over gradually. Offering a preview of the new version with a way back, the way large redesigns handle migration, converts a revolt into a transition.

**Aesthetic-usability effect.** People rate attractive interfaces as easier to use and forgive small usability problems in them. Kurosu and Kashimura at Hitachi, 1995: across 26 ATM layouts, rated ease of use tracked rated beauty more closely than it tracked actual ease of use. Visual quality buys real tolerance and trust. The effect masks problems in testing, so watch what people do, not the score they give a pretty prototype. And it only stretches so far: it forgives friction, not failure.

## Principles for the designer

**Occam's razor.** Among options that perform equally, take the one with the fewest assumptions and parts. William of Ockham, fourteenth century. The working method: remove elements one at a time until removing one more breaks the function, and treat that point, not the deadline, as done. The discipline is in "perform equally": the razor never licenses cutting things the task actually needs.

**Tesler's law.** Every system carries irreducible complexity; the only choice is who absorbs it. Larry Tesler at Xerox PARC, 1980s. His arithmetic still decides arguments: an engineer spending an extra week to remove a step beats millions of users each spending an extra minute on it. When complexity truly cannot be absorbed by the system, place it honestly and explain it, rather than hiding it where it will resurface as an error. Tognazzini's caveat is real too: simplify a tool and people promptly attempt harder things with it, so the complexity budget refills.

**Pareto principle.** Roughly 80 percent of effects come from 20 percent of causes. Vilfredo Pareto's land-ownership observation, generalized. In products: a few tasks carry most of the usage, a few defects cause most of the complaints, a few screens do most of the work. Find your product's actual 20 percent from data rather than from opinion, make it excellent, and be suspicious of any roadmap that spreads effort evenly. Misuse: quoting 80/20 without ever measuring which fifth is yours.

# Design ethics and deceptive patterns

Read this before shipping anything that shapes a choice: pricing, consent, cancellation, urgency, defaults, permissions, or subscriptions. Also read it if you build components that other people use to build interfaces.

The premise is simple. Every arrangement is a nudge and there is no neutral layout, so the question is never whether you are influencing a decision. It is whether the person would still make the same choice if they could see what you did. That is the test that survives contact with a real product meeting.

A useful definition: a deceptive pattern is an interface built with an understanding of human psychology to get someone to do something they did not intend to do. Note that deceptive patterns are usually well designed, often beautiful, and frequently effective. Ugly is not the tell.

## The catalog

Grouped by mechanism. Severity is about the harm and the legal exposure, not how obvious it is.

### Sneaking: hiding or delaying information a person would want

| Pattern | Severity | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Hidden costs | Critical | Fees, shipping, taxes, or service charges revealed at the last step |
| Sneak into basket | Critical | Warranties, insurance, donations, or add-ons in the cart the person did not select |
| Drip pricing | Critical | Advertised price that only assembles into the real price over several screens |
| Bait and switch | Critical | The advertised action produces a different outcome |
| Hidden subscription | Critical | A purchase that quietly enrolls in recurring billing |

### Urgency and social proof: manufacturing pressure

| Pattern | Severity | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Fabricated scarcity | Critical | "Only 2 left" not backed by real inventory |
| Fake countdown | Critical | A timer that resets on refresh or restarts for the next visitor |
| Fake activity | Critical | "14 people are viewing this" generated at random |
| Fake reviews or testimonials | Critical | Invented or incentivized without disclosure |
| Fake demand signals | Critical | Purchase popups, sales tickers, or trending badges generated by rule or randomness rather than read from real data |

Real scarcity and real deadlines are legitimate and often helpful. The line is whether the claim is bound to a fact.

### Interface interference: making one option easier to see or hit

| Pattern | Severity | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Visual misdirection | High | Decline styled weaker, smaller, or lower contrast than accept |
| Confirmshaming | High | "No thanks, I like paying full price" |
| Trick wording | High | Double negatives, or a checkbox where checking means opting out |
| Preselection | Critical | Consent, marketing, or data sharing on by default. Under GDPR Article 7 this is not valid consent |
| Disguised ads | High | Advertising styled as content or as a system control |
| False hierarchy | High | Presenting one option as the recommended default without a real basis |

### Obstruction: making the exit harder than the entrance

| Pattern | Severity | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Roach motel | Critical | Signup in two clicks, cancellation by phone during business hours |
| Opt-out burden | Critical | Cancellation harder than signup, which US rules on negative-option marketing target directly |
| Price comparison prevention | Medium | Structuring information so options cannot be compared side by side |
| Intermediate currency | Medium | Points or coins that obscure the real money cost |

### Forced action: extracting something for access

| Pattern | Severity | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Forced continuity | Critical | A trial that becomes a charge with no warning and no easy exit |
| Forced enrollment | High | Requiring an account, marketing consent, or contact sharing for something that does not need it |
| Privacy zuckering | Critical | Coaxing people into sharing more than they meant to |
| Nagging | Medium | Repeated interruptions for rating, notifications, or upgrade |

### Failure and feedback: the quiet ones

| Pattern | Severity | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Destructive defaults | High | Irreversible delete or publish too easy to trigger, no undo |
| Missing feedback | High | Action taken, nothing visibly happens, person repeats it |
| Broken error recovery | High | "An error occurred." and nothing else |
| Dead ends | Medium | An empty state or error page with no path forward |
| Mobile afterthought | High | A desktop-first surface that drops features or breaks on a phone |

These last five are usually negligence rather than intent. The harm is the same to the person on the other end, and they are usually cheaper to fix than the intentional ones.

## The regulations that actually bite

Not legal advice, and it changes. What matters is knowing which of these sit near your work so you can bring in someone who does give legal advice.

- **GDPR (EU/EEA and UK).** Consent must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous, which rules out pre-checked boxes and consent bundled with terms. Withdrawal has to be as easy as giving it. Data protection by design and by default is a legal obligation, not a best practice.
- **EU Digital Services Act.** Article 25 prohibits online platform interfaces that deceive, manipulate, or otherwise distort a user's ability to make free and informed decisions.
- **EU Digital Markets Act.** Consent and choice-architecture obligations for designated gatekeepers, including how choice screens and defaults are presented.
- **US FTC.** Acts against deceptive and unfair practices, and has been active on negative-option marketing, where the requirement is that cancellation be at least as easy as enrollment. Also active on fake reviews and on undisclosed fees.
- **US state privacy laws** including California, several of which explicitly say a consent interface built with a dark pattern does not produce valid consent.
- **Accessibility law** in most markets, referencing WCAG 2.1 or 2.2 at AA. Covered in `references/accessibility.md`.
- **Sector rules** on top of all of it: financial promotion rules, health data rules, rules about advertising to children, and platform store policies which are often stricter than the law.

Two things follow. First, "our competitor does it" is not a defense, since enforcement tends to arrive for a whole sector at once. Second, the person who ships the pattern usually is not the person who carries the liability, which is exactly why it needs to be a design review question and not only a legal one.

## If you build a platform

If merchants, marketers, or other developers build with your components, the question sharpens: which of these patterns does my platform make easy?

A countdown timer section, a stock scarcity badge, a pre-checked marketing consent at checkout, an announcement bar with a deadline. Each of those is a component someone will eventually ask you to build. Three positions, and pick one deliberately per component rather than by default:

1. **Do not ship the capability.** No fake-deadline timer at all.
2. **Ship it bound to truth.** A scarcity badge that can only read real inventory. A countdown that requires a real datetime and does not reset. Usually the right answer, because it serves the honest use and makes the dishonest one impossible.
3. **Ship it with the builder warned.** The weakest option, and only where the first two genuinely do not work.

The review question to put in your component checklist: could someone build a critical-severity pattern with this component in under a minute? If yes, position 1 or 2.

## What to do instead

Refusing a pattern is easier when you have the honest alternative ready.

| Instead of | Ship |
|---|---|
| Hidden fees | Total cost visible from the first step, including estimated tax and shipping |
| Fake urgency | Real inventory, real deadlines, or nothing |
| Fake social proof | Real reviews with the volume shown, including the bad ones |
| Pre-checked consent | Unchecked, with a clear statement of what each choice does |
| Roach motel | Cancel in the same number of clicks as signup, then ask why on the way out |
| Confirmshaming | A neutral decline of equal visual weight |
| Forced continuity | An email before the trial converts, and an easy pause option |
| Forced account creation | Guest checkout, with an offer to save details after the purchase |
| Nagging | Ask once, at a moment of demonstrated value, and remember the answer |

These generally perform worse on the immediate conversion metric and better on retention, refund rate, chargebacks, support cost, and app store rating. If someone insists on measuring, measure the whole set, and measure over a quarter rather than a week.

## How to raise it without losing the room

- Lead with exposure and cost, not with morality. "This is pre-checked consent, which is not valid consent under GDPR" moves faster than "this feels wrong."
- Bring the alternative in the same sentence you bring the objection.
- Ask for the retention number alongside the conversion number, since that is often the whole argument.
- Name the pattern. Named things are easier to decline than vague discomfort, and the naming is why the catalog exists.
- Write down the decision either way. If a team ships it anyway, a written record of the position taken and why is what makes it fixable later.
